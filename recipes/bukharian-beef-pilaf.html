Bukharian Beef Pilaf

Across the Silk Road, from Baku to Bukhara, pilaf is not a side dish — it is the meal. This version carries the hallmarks of the Bukharian Jewish kitchen: onions cooked until almost jammy, shredded carrot for sweetness, crushed walnuts for body, and a thread of cinnamon and silan that ties the whole thing together with a gentle, ancient warmth. The rice is cooked separately and served beneath or alongside the stew, each grain distinct, soaking up the sauce underneath.

Prep 20 min
Cook 1–3 hrs
Servings 4–6
Difficulty Easy

Ingredients

  • 2 cups basmati rice
  • 2 large onions, thinly sliced
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • 2 medium carrots, coarsely grated
  • ½ cup walnuts, roughly chopped
  • 3–4 cloves garlic, minced
  • 1 tsp turmeric
  • ¼ tsp cinnamon
  • 2 tbsp fruit jam (blueberry or cranberry)
  • 3 tbsp neutral oil
  • 1.5 lbs fatty beef (short rib, chuck, or lamb shoulder)
  • Salt and pepper to taste

On the jam: Blueberry gives a deeper, earthier sweetness while cranberry adds a slight tartness that cuts through the richness of the meat. Either works — avoid anything too sweet or artificial tasting.


No beef? This dish works beautifully with bone-in chicken thighs, firm tofu (pan-fried first), or no protein at all — the onion and walnut base is rich enough to stand on its own.

Instructions

  1. 01

    Prepare the meat

    Choose your method based on time. For the pressure cooker method, season the meat with salt and pepper and cook under pressure for 40–50 minutes until completely tender and falling apart. Reserve the cooking liquid. Alternatively, add the raw seasoned meat directly to the pot with the sauce in step 4 and braise, covered, on low heat for 2–3 hours. Both paths lead to the same place — the pressure cooker just gets you there faster.

  2. 02

    Caramelize the onions

    Heat the oil in a wide, heavy-bottomed pot over medium heat. Add the sliced onions and a pinch of salt and cook, stirring occasionally, for 20–25 minutes until deeply golden and soft. Add the sugar halfway through — it accelerates the caramelization and adds a subtle depth that is very characteristic of this kitchen. Don't rush this step. The onions are the foundation of everything.

  3. 03

    Add the carrot and walnuts

    Add the grated carrot to the golden onions and stir to combine. Cook for another 5 minutes until the carrot softens and begins to melt into the onion mixture. Add the chopped walnuts and stir through. The walnuts will toast lightly in the residual oil, giving the dish a nutty richness that runs throughout.

  4. 04

    Spice the pot

    Add the garlic, turmeric, and cinnamon and stir for 30 seconds until fragrant. Spoon in the jam and stir to coat everything evenly. Add a splash of water or the reserved meat cooking liquid and stir into a loose, fragrant sauce. If using raw meat or chicken thighs, add them now, nestle them into the mixture, cover, and braise on low heat until fully cooked through. If the meat is pre-cooked, shred or chunk it and add it here, letting it warm through in the sauce for 15–20 minutes.

  5. 05

    Cook the rice separately

    While the stew finishes, cook your basmati rice using the Persian method: rinse until the water runs clear, par-boil in heavily salted water for 6–7 minutes, drain, then steam on low heat with a wrapped lid for 30–35 minutes. The rice should be fluffy, with each grain separate. Cooking it apart from the stew keeps the texture clean and lets both components shine on the plate.

  6. 06

    Serve

    Mound the rice on a large platter and spoon the stew generously over the top, letting the sauce seep down into the rice. Alternatively, serve the stew alongside in a separate bowl and let people build their own plate. Either way, finish with a small spoonful of jam stirred through and a few extra walnut pieces on top if you want to dress it up.

Chef's note

The fruit jam might sound unexpected but it belongs here — it gives the sauce a dark, jammy sweetness that ties the cinnamon and turmeric together in a way that plain sugar never could. Cranberry is the bolder choice; blueberry is the more subtle one.
